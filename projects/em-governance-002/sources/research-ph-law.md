# Philippine law check: can Experimental Machines make "the OpenAI move"?

Research note for the Experimental Machines governance draft (non-stock, non-profit scientific ML research corporation aiming for NIRC Sec. 30(E) exemption). Compiled 2026-09-29.

**This is not legal advice.** It records what the statutes, regulations, SEC opinions and Supreme Court decisions say, where each was read, and what is still uncertain. A Philippine corporate and tax lawyer should confirm every point before anyone relies on it.

## How to read the tags

- **VERIFIED**: I read the primary text at the URL given, and the quote is copied from it.
- **VERIFIED (secondary)**: I read it only in a secondary source (law firm, tax adviser or NGO summary). The primary text was not reachable or not found.
- **UNVERIFIED**: I did not find a source, or the point is my own inference from verified texts. The reason is given.
- Quotes from Philippine statutes, regulations, SEC opinions and court decisions are government works (public domain). Secondary sources are paraphrased, not quoted.

## Q1. Can a non-stock corporation be converted into a stock corporation?

**Short answer: not by amending the articles. The SEC's position is that the non-stock corporation must first be dissolved and its assets liquidated under the non-stock distribution rules. After that, the former members may form a new stock corporation. Members never receive the non-stock corporation's assets as shares.**

1. **SEC-OGC Opinion No. 22-14 (7 October 2022), "Re: Legality of converting a non-stock, non-profit corporation to a stock corporation"**, addressed to Capt. Pol Winston G. Haboc for a 30-year-old non-stock maritime training school. **VERIFIED** (PDF read in browser).
   - Holding on questions 1 and 3: "A non-stock corporation may not be "converted" into a stock corporation without liquidating its assets nor is an undertaking in the Corporate By-laws indicating that the corporation is accountable for the obligations acquired while it was still a non-stock corporation sufficient."
   - It adopts the **SEC Opinion to Atty. Maria Clara Tankeh-Asuncion dated 20 March 1995**, quoted in the PDF:
     - "Until the corporation is dissolved and unless it is so provided in the articles of incorporation or by-laws, the members are not entitled to any beneficial or vested interest over the assets of a non-stock corporation. In other words, a non-stock, non-profit corporation only holds its funds in trust for carrying out the objectives and purposes expressed in its charter or articles of incorporation."
     - "The conversion of an existing "non-stock, non-profit corporation" into a "stock corporation" by mere amendment of the articles of incorporation would be tantamount to distribution of the corporate assets or income of the corporation to its members inasmuch as thereafter they automatically become stockholders thereof. This scheme might defraud the public who may have contributed donations, gifts or grants to the non-stock, non-profit corporation since after its conversion the donated corporate assets would in effect be treated as paid-in capital or subscription payments of the stockholders."
     - Conclusion drawn: "a non-stock corporation cannot be converted into a stock corporation by mere amendment of the articles of incorporation."
   - Holding on question 2 (conversion after liquidation): allowed only as dissolve-then-reincorporate. It quotes the **SEC-OGC Opinion to Mr. Conrado B. Roxas dated 24 February 1989**: "For purposes of transformation, it is fundamental that the non-stock corporation must be dissolved first under any of the methods specified in Title XIV of the Corporation Code so that the corporate assets shall be liquidated in accordance with the distribution procedure for non-stock corporations. Thereafter, the members thereof may organize a stock corporation directed to bring profits or pecuniary gains to themselves."
   - It also lists the characteristics of a non-stock corporation from **SEC-OGC Opinion No. 29-06 (7 June 2006, to Ms. Rayla Melchor Santos)**, including that "(b) It may not lawfully engage in any business activity for profit as it would run counter to its very nature as a non-profit entity" and "(c) When incidental to the objects and purposes of the corporation and without the end of making profits to be distributed to the members, it may engage in certain economic activities stated in its articles of incorporation".
   - URL (PDF): https://www.sec.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/2022OpinionNo22-14_Legalityofconvertinganon-stock_non-profittoastockcorporation.pdf
   - Page: https://www.sec.gov.ph/opinion-2022/opinion-no-22-14-re-legality-of-converting-a-non-stock-non-profit-corporation-to-a-stock-corporation/
2. The same holding is summarised by Ocampo & Suralvo, by Alburo law office, and in a third-party "SEC Legal Opinions Digest". **VERIFIED (secondary)**, consistent with the primary text. I did not read the 1995 and 1989 opinions themselves; they are known only as quoted inside 22-14.
3. **Is an SEC-OGC opinion binding?** Each opinion states that it "shall not be used in the nature of a standing rule binding upon the Commission in other cases or upon the courts whether of similar or dissimilar circumstances" (citing SEC MC 2003-15). **VERIFIED** (text of 22-14, 24-21, 24-33 and 25-13). These opinions show the SEC's consistent practice, not binding precedent.
4. **A merger of a non-stock into a stock corporation** would be a different route to the same result. The RCC merger provisions (Secs. 75-79) do not expressly exclude non-stock constituents. I found **no SEC opinion** on a non-stock corporation merging into a stock corporation. **UNVERIFIED.** 22-14's reasoning (conversion = distribution to members; the donor-fraud concern) would likely apply. That is my inference; ask counsel.
5. **A "stock, non-profit corporation" is a real category, but it is a different animal.** **SEC-OGC Opinion No. 25-13 (29 August 2025, posted 5 January 2026), "Re: Stock, Non-Profit Corporation"**, concerns Universidad de Zamboanga. It held that a corporation with capital stock remains a stock corporation even if its articles say no dividends will be paid. **VERIFIED.**
   - Quote: "notwithstanding Article 9 of its AOI, the status of UDZ as a stock corporation is not affected. Further, while UDZ's stockholders are not entitled to dividends, they still have an interest in the corporate assets upon dissolution as they are entitled to the repayment of the value of their shares upon such dissolution."
   - The opinion records the requester's statement that BIR taxes UDZ "as a proprietary corporation and assessing it for a preferential corporate income tax rate of 10%" (the Sec. 27(B) school rate).
   - URL: https://www.sec.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/2025Opinion_SEC-OGC-Opinion-No-25-13-Stock-Non-profit-Corporation.pdf
   - Such an entity cannot claim Sec. 30(E), which requires a **non-stock** corporation (see St. Luke's in Q3).

## Q2. Can a non-stock, non-profit corporation own shares in, or create, a for-profit company?

**Short answer: the RCC lets a non-stock corporation invest in "any other corporation". The SEC applies Sec. 41 to non-stock corporations. The SEC's separate rule is that a non-stock corporation's business activities must be incidental to its stated purpose. I found no SEC opinion squarely on a non-stock corporation controlling a for-profit subsidiary.**

1. **RCC Sec. 35(g)**: every corporation has power "To purchase, receive, take or grant, hold, convey, sell, lease, pledge, mortgage, and otherwise deal with such real and personal property, including securities and bonds of other corporations, as the transaction of the lawful business of the corporation may reasonably and necessarily require, subject to the limitations prescribed by law and the constitution". **VERIFIED** (lawphil).
2. **RCC Sec. 41** (full text): "Subject to the provisions of this Code, a private corporation may invest its funds in any other corporation, business, or for any purpose other than the primary purpose for which it was organized, when approved by a majority of the board of directors or trustees and ratified by the stockholders representing at least two-thirds (2/3) of the outstanding capital stock, or by at least two-thirds (2/3) of the members in the case of nonstock corporations, at a meeting duly called for the purpose. [notice provisions] ... Provided, That any dissenting stockholder shall have appraisal right as provided in this Code: Provided, however, That where the investment by the corporation is reasonably necessary to accomplish its primary purpose as stated in the articles of incorporation, the approval of the stockholders or members shall not be necessary." **VERIFIED** (Supreme Court E-Library text).
   - Thresholds for a non-stock corporation:
     - Investment outside the primary purpose: majority of trustees, **plus** ratification by 2/3 of members at a meeting called for the purpose.
     - Investment "reasonably necessary" to the primary purpose: trustees' approval alone.
   - The section names non-stock corporations expressly, so it applies to them.
3. **SEC-OGC Opinion No. 24-33 (5 November 2024; web page dated 21 November 2024)**, to Atty. Geneses R. Abot for a non-stock microfinance NGO. It applied Sec. 41 to a non-stock corporation's proposed transfer of funds to its international affiliate. **VERIFIED.**
   - Quote: "where the investment by the corporation is reasonably necessary to accomplish its primary purpose as stated in its articles of incorporation, only the approval of the Board of the corporation is necessary. However, if the investment of funds is made in any other corporation or business or for any purpose other than the primary purpose for which the investing corporation was organized, the ratification of the stockholders representing at least two-thirds (2/3) of the outstanding capital stock or by at least two-thirds (2/3) of the members in case of non-stock corporation must also be complied with."
   - It concluded: "it is within the corporation's business judgment to determine what legitimate purposes are to be undertaken by it, provided Section 41 is complied with".
   - URL: https://www.sec.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/2024Opinion_SEC-OGC-Opinion-No.-24-33-Re-Foreigner-in-a-Mirco-Finance-Non-Governmental-Organization-the-Anti-Dummy-Law-and-the-power-to-invest-Corporate-Funds.pdf
   - The SEC web page is mislabelled "Opinion No. 24-29". The PDF says 24-33.
4. **SEC-OGC Opinion No. 24-21 (16 August 2024; web page dated 9 October 2024)**, for World Vision Development Foundation (a non-stock foundation) running a contact centre that serves its foreign affiliates. **VERIFIED.**
   - Rule stated: "non-stock, non-profit corporations, as a general rule, are not empowered to venture on profitable business activities. By way of exception, the corporation may, as incident to its purpose(s), engage in business activities which are reasonably necessary to carry out the purpose(s) for which the corporation was organized." It adds: "If the business activity is not incidental, necessary or essential to carry out the express objectives of the corporation as appearing in its AOI, the same cannot be undertaken by a non-stock corporation."
   - Answer: yes, "for as long as" (1) the service "is reasonable and not primarily profit-making, and is limited to its World Vision affiliates" and (2) "any profit that may be derived therefrom are not distributable to the members but are used for the furtherance of corporate purposes."
   - URL: https://www.sec.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/2024Opinion-No-24-21-re-Non-Stock-Non-Profit-Org-to-Establish-Income-Generating-Activity-Contact-Call-Center.pdf
5. **SEC-OGC Opinion No. 26-08 (31 March 2026), "Loan Program of a Non-Stock, Non-Profit Alumni Association"**, restates the purpose-clause test and adds a warning about personal benefit. **VERIFIED.**
   - The test: "If the business activity is not incidental, necessary or essential to carry out the express objectives of the corporation as appearing in its AOI, the same cannot be undertaken by a non-stock corporation."
   - It rejected a member-loan scheme that "partakes the proscribed distribution of profits or income to the members prior to dissolution".
   - It quotes an earlier opinion (SEC-OGC 31-11, 13 July 2011): "It is only upon dissolution when the members can reap for themselves any incidental income or profit made by non-stock corporations".
   - URL: https://www.sec.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/2026Opinion_SEC-OGC-Opinion-No-26-08.pdf
6. **RR 13-98 Sec. 1(c)(v)** (tax regulation for accredited donee NGOs) counts as "utilization" "Any amount in cash or in kind invested in capital sustaining and generating activities, such as but not limited to, endowment funds, trust funds, money market placements, shares of stock and similar instruments: Provided, That, any income derived from these investments shall be exclusively used in activities directly related to one or more purposes for which the accredited NGO was created or organized." **VERIFIED** (PCNC-hosted copy). This shows the BIR contemplates donee NGOs holding shares as investments.
7. **Not found:** an SEC or BIR issuance that specifically addresses a non-stock corporation **creating and controlling** an operating for-profit subsidiary (as opposed to holding investment shares). **UNVERIFIED.** A secondary source (the ICNL/Council on Foundations country note, current as of June 2026) says NPOs may not engage *primarily* in business activities and that any profit must go to their objectives. **VERIFIED (secondary).**

## Q3. Tax consequences under NIRC Sec. 30, St. Luke's, YMCA, and dividends, royalties and rent

### The statute

- **NIRC Sec. 30(E)** exempts "Nonstock corporation or association organized and operated exclusively for religious, charitable, scientific, athletic, or cultural purposes, or for the rehabilitation of veterans, no part of its net income or asset shall belong to or inures to the benefit of any member, organizer, officer or any specific person". **VERIFIED** (lawphil RA 8424).
- **Last paragraph of Sec. 30**: "Notwithstanding the provisions in the preceding paragraphs, the income of whatever kind and character of the foregoing organizations from any of their properties, real or personal, or from any of their activities conducted for profit regardless of the disposition made of such income, shall be subject to tax imposed under this Code." **VERIFIED** (lawphil; quoted identically in St. Luke's 2012).
- Sec. 30 opens: organizations "shall not be taxed **under this Title** in respect to income received by them as such". Title II is income tax. **VERIFIED.** So the exemption does not reach donor's tax (Title III), VAT or withholding duties.
- **Sec. 30 was not amended** by TRAIN (RA 10963), CREATE (RA 11534) or CMEPA (RA 12214). **VERIFIED** by searching the full texts of all three on lawphil for amendments to Sec. 30. None found.

### CIR v. St. Luke's Medical Center, G.R. No. 195909, 26 September 2012 (Second Division, Carpio, J.)

**VERIFIED.** https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2012/sep2012/gr_195909_2012.html

- Four requirements of Sec. 30(E): "(1) A non-stock corporation or association; (2) Organized exclusively for charitable purposes; (3) Operated exclusively for charitable purposes; and (4) No part of its net income or asset shall belong to or inure to the benefit of any member, organizer, officer or any specific person."
- Profits must be used for the purpose: "under Lung Center, any profit by a charitable institution must not only be plowed back "whenever necessary or proper," but must be "devoted or used altogether to the charitable object which it is intended to achieve.""
- Core holding: "if a tax exempt charitable institution conducts "any" activity for profit, such activity is not tax exempt even as its not-for-profit activities remain tax exempt."
- And: "An institution under Section 30(E) or (G) does not lose its tax exemption if it earns income from its for-profit activities. Such income from for-profit activities, under the last paragraph of Section 30, is merely subject to income tax, previously at the ordinary corporate rate but now at the preferential 10% rate pursuant to Section 27(B)."
- On using the non-stock form: "An institution cannot use its corporate form to prevent its profitable activities from being taxed."
- The 10% rate applied only because St. Luke's is a proprietary non-profit **hospital** under Sec. 27(B). Sec. 27(B) covers only "Proprietary educational institutions and hospitals" (**VERIFIED**, CREATE text). A research nonprofit would therefore pay the regular corporate rate on its for-profit income: 25%, or 20% for small corporations under CREATE's Sec. 27(A). **UNVERIFIED as applied**; this is my inference from the statutory text.

### CIR v. Court of Appeals, CTA and YMCA, G.R. No. 124043, 14 October 1998 (First Division, Panganiban, J.)

**VERIFIED.** https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1998/oct1998/gr_124043_1998.html

- Facts: YMCA earned rent from shops and canteens and parking fees. The CTA and CA had held these "reasonably incidental" and exempt. The Supreme Court reversed.
- Holding: "The phrase "any of their activities conducted for profit" does not qualify the word "properties." This makes [income] from the property of the organization taxable, regardless of how that income is used whether for profit or for lofty non-profit purposes."
- And: "The rental income is taxable regardless of whence such income is derived and how it is used or disposed of. Where the law does not distinguish, neither should we."

### BIR's own reading (RMO 38-2019, Part V)

**VERIFIED.**

- "Income tax exemption covers only the income derived by the corporation in furtherance of the purposes for which it was organized under Section 30".
- Sec. 30 organizations remain taxable on "income derived from any of their properties, real or personal, or any activity conducted for profit regardless of the disposition thereof (i.e. interest income from bank deposits, gains from investments, rental income from real or personal properties), which income should be reported for taxation purposes."
- "royalties derived from sources within the Philippines of organizations under Section 30 are subject to the 20% final withholding tax".
- VAT: "if the corporation is engaged in the sale of goods or services in the course of a business pursuit, including transactions incidental thereto, its revenues derived therefrom shall be subject to the 12% VAT, in case the gross receipts from such sales exceed Three Million Pesos (3,000,000.00), or to the 3% percentage tax" if not.

### Intercorporate dividends: does the exemption apply to a non-stock parent?

**Status: UNCERTAIN.**

1. **The provision (now Sec. 27(D)(3), after CMEPA).**
   - CREATE (RA 11534) wording: "Dividends received by a domestic corporation shall not be subject to tax under this title: Provided, That for foreign-sourced dividends to be exempt, the funds from such dividends actually received or remitted into the Philippines are reinvested in the business operations of the domestic corporation in the Philippines within the next taxable year ... Provided, further, That the domestic corporation holds directly at least twenty percent (20%) of the outstanding shares of the foreign corporation and has held the shareholdings for a minimum of two (2) years at the time of the dividends distribution." **VERIFIED** (lawphil RA 11534).
   - CMEPA (RA 12214, 29 May 2025) renumbered the paragraph to 27(D)(3) and left its text as "x x x", meaning unchanged. **VERIFIED** (lawphil RA 12214).
   - The founders' draft cites "27(D)(4)". Update it to **27(D)(3)**.
2. **Is a non-stock corporation a "domestic corporation"?**
   - NIRC Sec. 22(B): "The term 'corporation' shall include partnerships, no matter how created or organized, joint-stock companies, joint accounts ..., association, or insurance companies".
   - Sec. 22(C): "'domestic,' when applied to a corporation, means created or organized in the Philippines or under its laws."
   - **VERIFIED** (text). Nothing in these definitions excludes non-stock corporations. So on the text, dividends from a domestic subsidiary to a Philippine non-stock parent fall within the exemption.
3. **Counter-reading.** The Sec. 30 last paragraph taxes income "from any of their properties", and shares are personal property. But it taxes that income only as "subject to tax imposed under this Code". The tax the Code imposes on domestic intercorporate dividends is nil. The two provisions look consistent: the dividend is exempt either way. **This is my inference. I found no BIR ruling, RMO or court decision confirming it.**
4. **Foreign-sourced dividends** (e.g., from a US or Singapore for-profit) are exempt only if the CREATE conditions are met (reinvestment, 20% holding, 2-year holding). How those conditions work for a Sec. 30(E) organization is **UNVERIFIED**.
5. **The bigger dividend risk is operational, not tax-rate.** The more the nonprofit's activity becomes running or financing a business, the more it risks failing the RMO 38-2019 operational test (Q4).

## Q4. RMO 38-2019: tests, inurement, revocation, commercial activity

**Revenue Memorandum Order No. 38-2019, "Tax Exemption of Non-Stock, Non-Profit Corporations Under Section 30 of the National Internal Revenue Code of 1997, As Amended"**, signed by CIR Caesar R. Dulay, issued 24 July 2019 (date per BIR digest).

- Full text: https://www.pcnc.com.ph/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/BIR-RMO-38-2019.pdf (**VERIFIED**)
- BIR's own digest: https://bir-cdn.bir.gov.ph/BIR/pdf/RMO_NO.38-2019_digest.pdf (**VERIFIED**)

1. **Sec. 30(E) "scientific purposes" (II.E.2.c)**: "refers to undertaking or assisting in pure or basic, applied and scientific research in the field of agriculture, forestry, fisheries, industry, engineering, energy development, food and nutrition, medicine, environment and biological, physical and natural sciences **for the public interest**." **VERIFIED.**
   - Computer science and ML are not named in the list. Whether ML research fits "industry, engineering ... physical and natural sciences" is **UNVERIFIED**. Ask counsel how to word the purpose clause.
   - Actual operation (II.E.3): "A corporation is exempt from tax on its income if it meets two tests: (a) It is organized and operated for one or more of the above-specified purposes; and (b) no part of its net income or assets inures to the benefit of private stockholders or individuals."
2. **Organizational test (III)**: "This requires that the corporation or association's constitutive documents (SEC Registration, Articles of Incorporation and By-Laws) must show that its primary purpose/s of incorporation fall under Section 30 of the NIRC." **VERIFIED.**
3. **Operational test (III)**: "This requires that the regular activities of the corporation or association be exclusively devoted to the accomplishment of the purposes specified in Section 30 of the NIRC. A corporation or association fails to meet this test if the corporation has no activities conducted in furtherance of the purpose for which it was organized, or if a substantial part of its operations constitutes "activities conducted for profit"." **VERIFIED.**
   - Tension to flag: St. Luke's says for-profit activity is merely taxed and the exemption survives for the rest. The RMO says a "substantial part" of for-profit operations fails the test altogether. How the two fit together is an open question for counsel.
4. **Definition of non-profit and inurement (IV)**. **VERIFIED.**
   - "Non-profit" means "no net income or asset accrues to or benefits any member or specific person, with all the net income or asset devoted to the institution's purposes and all its activities conducted not for profit".
   - The entity "must demonstrate that its earnings or assets do not inure to the benefit of any of its trustees, organizers, officers, members or any specific person. It must not be organized or operated for the benefit of private interests such as specific individuals, incorporators or his family, shareholders of the organization, **or persons controlled directly or indirectly by such private interests**. The organization must serve a public rather than a private purpose."
   - A for-profit company controlled by the founders is squarely a "person controlled directly or indirectly by such private interests".
5. **The six listed "inurements" (IV.A)**. **VERIFIED.**
   1. "The payment of compensation, salaries, or honorarium to its trustees or organizers;"
   2. "The payment of exorbitant or unreasonable compensation to its employees;"
   3. "The provision of welfare aid and financial assistance to its members. ..."
   4. "Donation to any person or entity (except donations made to other entities formed for the purpose/purposes similar to its own);"
   5. "The purchase of goods or services for amounts in excess of the fair market value of such goods or value of such services from an entity in which one or more of its trustees, officers or fiduciaries have an interest; and"
   6. "When upon dissolution and satisfaction of all liabilities, its remaining assets are distributed to its trustees, organizers, officers or members. Its assets must be dedicated to its exempt purpose. Accordingly, its constitutive documents must expressly provide that in the event of dissolution, its assets shall be distributed to one or more entities formed for the purpose/purposes similar to its own, or to the Philippine government for public purpose."
   - Item 1 matters for the draft. The draft treats the founders as "organizers", and on the RMO's plain words any salary to them is listed inurement, even as employee-researchers. RCC Sec. 29 bars trustee compensation "in their capacity as such" only. An SEC opinion summarised in the digest says trustees may be paid in another capacity. The BIR list has no such carve-out. **Open question for counsel.**
6. **Required articles clauses (VI.A.2)**: the AOI "must expressly state":
   - "i. It is a non-stock, non-profit corporation or association;"
   - "ii. No part of the corporation or association's net income shall inure to the benefit of any private individual;"
   - "iii. The trustees of the non-profit corporation or association do not receive any compensation or remuneration; and"
   - "iv. In case of dissolution, its assets shall be distributed to one or more entities formed for the purpose/purposes similar to its own, or to the Philippine government for public purpose."
   - **VERIFIED.**
7. **Application must disclose (VI.A.3)** "(i) full description of its past, present, and proposed activities, (ii) the sources and disposition of income, (iii) detailed description of all revenues which it seeks to be exempted from income tax, and (iv) amount of income, compensation, salaries or any emoluments paid by the corporation or association to its trustees, officers and other executive officers." **VERIFIED.**
8. **Validity and revocation (VII)**: "A CTE issued under this Order shall be valid for a period of three (3) years from the date of effectivity specified in the Ruling, unless sooner revoked or cancelled." And: "The Tax Exemption Ruling shall be deemed revoked if there are material changes in the character, purpose, or method of operation of the corporation or association which are inconsistent with the basis for its income tax exemption. **The revocation takes effect as of the date of the material change.**" **VERIFIED.**
   - Creating or spinning into a for-profit arm is the kind of change in "method of operation" that could trigger this, with retroactive effect to the date of the change. That application is my inference.
9. **Affiliates**: the RMO has no section on affiliates or subsidiaries as such. Affiliates enter through the "persons controlled directly or indirectly by such private interests" language and inurement items 4-5. **VERIFIED** (absence confirmed by reading the full text).
10. **Later clarification: RMC No. 123-2024 (15 November 2024).** Per Grant Thornton's summary, it confirms that Sec. 30(E) organizations stay on the 3-year CTE cycle, unlike non-stock schools, homeowners' associations and some others whose CTEs remain valid until revoked. **VERIFIED (secondary):** https://www.grantthornton.com.ph/insights/articles-and-updates1/tax-notes/clarification-on-the-validity-of-certificates-of-tax-exemption-issued-to-certain-corporations/ . I did not read the primary RMC.

## Q5. RCC Secs. 31, 32, 33 (and 30) when the same three founders sit on both boards

Texts are from the Supreme Court E-Library copy of RA 11232. **VERIFIED.** https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/2/86463

**Sec. 31, Dealings of Directors, Trustees or Officers with the Corporation:**

"A contract of the corporation with one (1) or more of its directors, trustees, officers or their spouses and relatives within the fourth civil degree of consanguinity or affinity is voidable, at the option of such corporation, unless all the following conditions are present:
(a) The presence of such director or trustee in the board meeting in which the contract was approved was not necessary to constitute a quorum for such meeting;
(b) The vote of such director or trustee was not necessary for the approval of the contract;
(c) The contract is fair and reasonable under the circumstances;
(d) In case of corporations vested with public interest, material contracts are approved by at least two-thirds (2/3) of the entire membership of the board, with at least a majority of the independent directors voting to approve the material contract; and
(e) In case of an officer, the contract has been previously authorized by the board of directors.
Where any of the first three (3) conditions set forth in the preceding paragraph is absent, in the case of a contract with a director or trustee, such contract may be ratified by the vote of the stockholders representing at least two-thirds (2/3) of the outstanding capital stock or of at least two-thirds (2/3) of the members in a meeting called for the purpose: Provided, That full disclosure of the adverse interest of the directors or trustees involved is made at such meeting and the contract is fair and reasonable under the circumstances."

**Sec. 32, Contracts Between Corporations with Interlocking Directors:**

"Except in cases of fraud, and provided the contract is fair and reasonable under the circumstances, a contract between two (2) or more corporations having interlocking directors shall not be invalidated on that ground alone: Provided, That if the interest of the interlocking director in one (1) corporation is substantial and the interest in the other corporation or corporations is merely nominal, the contract shall be subject to the provisions of the preceding section insofar as the latter corporation or corporations are concerned.
Stockholdings exceeding twenty percent (20%) of the outstanding capital stock shall be considered substantial for purposes of interlocking directors."

**Sec. 33, Disloyalty of a Director:**

"Where a director, by virtue of such office, acquires a business opportunity which should belong to the corporation, thereby obtaining profits to the prejudice of such corporation, the director must account for and refund to the latter all such profits, unless the act has been ratified by a vote of the stockholders owning or representing at least two-thirds (2/3) of the outstanding capital stock. This provision shall be applicable, notwithstanding the fact that the director risked one's own funds in the venture."

**Sec. 30, second paragraph** (this one expressly names trustees):

"A director, trustee or officer shall not attempt to acquire, or acquire any interest adverse to the corporation in respect of any matter which has been reposed in them in confidence, and upon which, equity imposes a disability upon themselves to deal in their own behalf; otherwise, the said director, trustee or officer shall be liable as a trustee for the corporation and must account for the profits which otherwise would have accrued to the corporation."

**RCC Sec. 86, second paragraph**: "The provisions governing stock corporations, when pertinent, shall be applicable to nonstock corporations, except as may be covered by specific provisions of this Title." This is how Sec. 33 (written for "director" and "stockholders") would reach trustees and members. **VERIFIED** text; applying it to Sec. 33 is my inference.

**How these bite in the founders' setup** (my analysis from the texts; **UNVERIFIED** as to how SEC or courts would apply it):

1. **Sec. 32's 20% rule points to Sec. 31 on the nonprofit side.** If each founder holds more than 20% of the for-profit, their interest there is "substantial" by statute. Their interest in the non-stock nonprofit, where they hold no shares, is at most "nominal". The proviso then makes every contract between the two companies "subject to the provisions of the preceding section [Sec. 31] insofar as" the nonprofit is concerned.
2. **With three trustees who are all conflicted, Sec. 31(a) and (b) cannot be met.** Each founder's presence is needed for quorum and each vote is needed for approval. The contract is voidable at the nonprofit's option unless ratified by **2/3 of the members** at a meeting called for the purpose, with full disclosure, and the contract must still be "fair and reasonable".
3. **Ratification by the same people is weak.** If the three founders are also the only members, they would be ratifying their own deal. The statute does not forbid it, but it defeats the purpose. **RCC Sec. 91**: "Except with respect to independent trustees of nonstock corporations vested with public interest, only a member of the corporation shall be elected as trustee." (**VERIFIED**.) So adding independent people means adding them as members and then electing them as trustees.
4. **Corporate opportunity.** An opportunity that arose in the nonprofit's research (a model, a dataset, a customer) and was taken by the for-profit risks Sec. 30 para. 2 and Sec. 33. The founders would have to account for the profits, even though they risked their own money in the venture.
5. **Management contracts (RCC Sec. 43).** If either company will manage the other, the stricter vote applies "where a majority if the members of the board of directors of the managing corporation also constitute a majority of the members of the board of directors of the managed corporation": 2/3 of the managed corporation's stock or members. No term may exceed 5 years. **VERIFIED** (lawphil text).
6. **Transfers of "all or substantially all" assets (RCC Sec. 39)** need "at least two-thirds (2/3) of the members". A transfer counts as "substantially all" "if thereby the corporation would be rendered incapable of continuing the business or accomplishing the purpose of which it was incorporated". Moving the core model, IP or compute into the for-profit could qualify. **VERIFIED** (lawphil text); the application is my inference.

## Q6. RCC Secs. 86-87 and 93-94

- **Sec. 86**: "For purposes of this Code and subject to its provisions on dissolution, a nonstock corporation is one where no part of its income is distributable as dividends to its members, trustees, or officers: Provided, That any profit which a nonstock corporation may obtain incidental to its operations shall, whenever necessary or proper, be used for the furtherance of the purpose or purposes for which the corporation was organized, subject to the provisions of this Title." **VERIFIED** (E-Library).
- **Sec. 87**: non-stock corporations "may be formed or organized for charitable, religious, educational, professional, cultural, fraternal, literary, scientific, social, civic service, or similar purposes, like trade, industry, agricultural and like chambers, or any combination thereof". **VERIFIED** (lawphil; minor punctuation typos in that copy).
- **Sec. 93, Rules of Distribution** (for dissolution other than under Sec. 139). **VERIFIED** (E-Library):
  - "(a) All liabilities and obligations of the corporation shall be paid, satisfied and discharged, or adequate provision shall be made therefor;
  - (b) Assets held by the corporation upon a condition requiring return, transfer or conveyance, and which condition occurs by reason of the dissolution, shall be returned, transferred or conveyed in accordance with such requirements;
  - (c) Assets received and held by the corporation subject to limitations permitting their use only for charitable, religious, benevolent, educational or similar purposes, but not held upon a condition requiring return, transfer or conveyance by reason of the dissolution, shall be transferred or conveyed to one (1) or more corporations, societies or organizations engaged in activities in the Philippines substantially similar to those of the dissolving corporation according to a plan of distribution adopted pursuant to this Chapter;
  - (d) Assets other than those mentioned in the preceding paragraphs, if any, shall be distributed in accordance with the provisions of the articles of incorporation or the bylaws, to the extent that the articles of incorporation or the bylaws determine the distributive rights of members, or any class or classes of members, or provide for distribution; and
  - (e) In any other case, assets may be distributed to such persons, societies, organizations or corporations, **whether or not organized for profit**, as may be specified in a plan of distribution adopted pursuant to this Chapter."
- **Sec. 94**: a plan of distribution is adopted by majority of trustees, written notice to voting members, and approval by "at least two-thirds (2/3) of the members having voting rights present or represented by proxy at such meeting." **VERIFIED** (lawphil).

**Correction to the founders' draft.** The draft says "dissolution assets must go to similar nonprofits (RCC Secs. 93-94)". The RCC alone does not say that.

- Only **restricted-purpose** assets (Sec. 93(c)) must go to similar Philippine organizations.
- Other assets follow the articles or bylaws (93(d)) or a plan that may name persons or entities "whether or not organized for profit" (93(e)).
- The SEC reads Sec. 86 the same way: "It is only upon dissolution when the members can reap for themselves any incidental income or profit made by non-stock corporations" (SEC-OGC 26-08, quoting SEC-OGC 31-11). 22-14 likewise speaks of members' interest arising "Until the corporation is dissolved and unless it is so provided in the articles".

The "similar nonprofits only" lock actually comes from:

1. **Tax rules**: RMO 38-2019 IV.A.6 and VI.A.2.iv; for accredited donees, RR 13-98 Sec. 1(b)(iii) and Sec. 3(2)(iii). RR 13-98 requires distribution "to another accredited NGO organized for similar purpose or purposes, or to the State for public purpose".
2. **The AOI clause itself.** Once the AOI contains the clause, RCC 93(d) enforces it.

So the draft should cite RCC 93(c)-(d) **together with** RMO 38-2019, and the AOI dissolution clause is the piece that actually binds.

## Q7. Moving IP or assets from the nonprofit to a for-profit

1. **Donor's tax on below-value transfers.**
   - **NIRC Sec. 98(A)**: tax on "the transfer by any person, resident or nonresident, of the property by gift". Sec. 98(B): "whether the gift is direct or indirect, and whether the property is real or personal, tangible or intangible."
   - Sec. 22(A): "person" includes a corporation.
   - **VERIFIED** (lawphil RA 8424).
2. **NIRC Sec. 100 (as amended by TRAIN)**: "Where property, other than real property referred to in Section 24(D), is transferred for less than an adequate and full consideration in money or money's worth, then the amount by which the fair market value of the property exceeded the value of the consideration shall ... be deemed a gift ...: Provided, however, That a sale, exchange, or other transfer of property made in the ordinary course of business (a transaction which is a bona fide, at arm's length, and free from any donative intent), will be considered as made for an adequate and full consideration". **VERIFIED** (lawphil RA 10963).
3. **Rate (Sec. 99(A), TRAIN)**: "six percent (6%) computed on the basis of the total gifts in excess of Two hundred fifty thousand pesos (₱250,000) exempt gift made during the calendar year." **VERIFIED.**
4. **Applying this to the nonprofit.** A below-value transfer of IP, model weights or compute by the nonprofit to the for-profit would:
   - be a deemed gift by the nonprofit, taxable to it. Its Sec. 30 exemption covers only Title II income tax.
   - also be listed inurement: RMO IV.A.4, "Donation to any person or entity (except ... similar purpose)".
   - expose the trustees under RCC Sec. 30-31.
   - This combination is my inference from verified texts. **UNVERIFIED** as to any BIR ruling on this exact fact pattern.
5. **Fair value alone may not be enough for accredited donees. RR 13-98 Sec. 10 (Prohibited Transactions)** bars any accredited donee from:
   - "(b) Purchasing any security and/or property for more than an adequate consideration in money or money's worth;"
   - "(c) Selling any part of the security or other property for less than adequate consideration in money or money's worth;"
   - "(d) Diverting its income or transferring its property by way of lease or sale to any member of its Board of Trustees, founder/s or principal officers or any member of their families or to any corporation controlled directly or indirectly by the aforesaid individuals or their families in accordance with the attribution of stock ownership under Section 73 (A) and (B) of the Tax Code;"
   - "(e) Using any part of its property, income or seed capital for any purpose other than that for which the corporation was created or organized".
   - **VERIFIED:** https://www.pcnc.com.ph/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/BIR-RR-13-1998.pdf
   - Item (d) has no fair-price exception. It reaches any lease or sale to a corporation controlled by the founders.
   - Sanctions (Sec. 11): violation is ground for withdrawal of PCNC accreditation and BIR revocation of donee registration. A donor who "participated in or consented to the violation" loses deductibility and is assessed donor's tax.
   - RR 13-98 applies to **PCNC-accredited donee institutions**, not to every Sec. 30(E) CTE holder. It matters if Experimental Machines seeks donee status so donors can deduct gifts.
6. **BIR can reallocate income between commonly controlled entities. NIRC Sec. 50**: "In the case of two or more organizations, trades or businesses (whether or not incorporated and whether or not organized in the Philippines) owned or controlled directly or indirectly by the same interests, the Commissioner is authorized to distribute, apportion or allocate gross income or deductions between or among such organization, trade or business, if he determined that such distribution, apportionment or allocation is necessary in order to prevent evasion of taxes or clearly to reflect the income of any such organization". **VERIFIED** (lawphil).
7. **Donor restrictions.**
   - Assets held on a condition of return, or restricted to charitable or similar use, must be returned or conveyed to similar organizations on dissolution (RCC 93(b)-(c), above).
   - The SEC's anti-conversion reasoning rests on protecting "the public who may have contributed donations, gifts or grants" (22-14).
   - Gifts **to** the nonprofit are donor's-tax-exempt under **NIRC Sec. 101(A)(2) (TRAIN)** only if the donee is "incorporated as a nonstock entity, paying no dividends, governed by trustees who receive no compensation, and devoting all its income ... to the accomplishment and promotion of the purposes enumerated in its Articles of Incorporation", with "not more than thirty percent (30%) of said gifts" used for administration. **VERIFIED.** Moving donated assets to a for-profit would undercut the basis of that exemption. That is my inference.
8. **The public-interest condition on scientific research. RR 13-98 Sec. 1(g)(iii)**: "Scientific research will be regarded as carried on for public interest if the results of such research are made available to the public on a non-discriminatory basis; or if such research is performed for the Government ...; or if such research is directed to benefit the public." **VERIFIED.**
   - An exclusive licence of research results to the founders' company is in tension with "non-discriminatory" availability.
   - RMO 38-2019 also requires scientific research "for the public interest".
   - The application is my inference.
9. **Sale of IP at fair value.** The gain is income from "properties", so it is taxable under the Sec. 30 last paragraph (YMCA; RMO V.A.2 "gains from investments"). Whether an isolated IP sale is also subject to VAT depends on whether it is "in the course of trade or business" (RMO V.C). **UNVERIFIED** for this fact pattern.

## Legal caveats for "the OpenAI move" in the Philippines

1. **Converting in place is off the table.** The SEC's position is that a non-stock corporation cannot become a stock corporation by amendment. It must dissolve and liquidate first, because conversion would hand donated assets to members as "paid-in capital" (SEC-OGC 22-14, adopting the 1995 and 1989 opinions). Restricted assets then go to similar Philippine organizations (RCC 93(c)). Under a Sec. 30(E)-compliant AOI and RMO 38-2019 IV.A.6, all remaining assets must too.
2. **The founders cannot end up owning the nonprofit's value.** Members have no "beneficial or vested interest over the assets" before dissolution (22-14). Any distribution to trustees, organizers or members on dissolution is listed inurement (RMO 38-2019 IV.A.6). A US-style recapitalization that gives founders equity in exchange for the nonprofit's assets has no clean Philippine path.
3. **Owning a for-profit subsidiary is legally possible but gated.**
   - Power: RCC 35(g) and 41.
   - Votes: majority of trustees plus 2/3 of members, unless the investment is "reasonably necessary" to the primary purpose (RCC 41; SEC-OGC 24-33).
   - Scope: business activity must stay "incidental, necessary or essential" to the stated purpose (SEC-OGC 24-21, 26-08; 22-14 citing 29-06).
4. **Tax leakage and exemption risk.**
   - Rent is taxable whatever it is used for (YMCA 1998).
   - For-profit activity income is taxable (St. Luke's 2012), at the regular rate for a research nonprofit (Sec. 27(B) covers only schools and hospitals).
   - Royalties carry a 20% final tax (RMO 38-2019 V.A.3; NIRC 27(D)(6) after CMEPA).
   - If a "substantial part" of operations becomes for-profit, the operational test fails (RMO 38-2019 III), and the CTE is "deemed revoked" retroactively to the date of the material change (RMO VII).
5. **Dividends from the subsidiary are an open question.** The intercorporate dividend exemption (now NIRC 27(D)(3)) covers "a domestic corporation". On the text that includes a non-stock one (NIRC 22(B)-(C)). I found no BIR ruling applying it to a Sec. 30(E) parent. Foreign-sourced dividends carry extra conditions (CREATE).
6. **The same three founders on both boards cannot approve deals between the two companies.**
   - Holding more than 20% of the for-profit makes their interest "substantial" (RCC 32). Sec. 31 then governs on the nonprofit side.
   - With three conflicted trustees, Sec. 31(a)-(b) fail. Every contract is voidable unless 2/3 of members ratify after full disclosure and the contract is fair.
   - Corporate-opportunity rules (RCC 30 para. 2; 33 via 86) make them account for profits from opportunities that belonged to the nonprofit.
7. **Asset or IP transfers to a founder-controlled company are dangerous even at fair value.**
   - Below value, the transfer is a deemed gift, with 6% donor's tax on the nonprofit (NIRC 98-100) and inurement (RMO IV.A.4).
   - For PCNC-accredited donees, any sale or lease to "any corporation controlled directly or indirectly by" founders or trustees is a prohibited transaction regardless of price (RR 13-98 Sec. 10(d)). The sanction is loss of accreditation, and donors lose their deductions.
   - BIR may reallocate income between commonly controlled entities (NIRC 50).
   - A transfer of "substantially all" assets needs 2/3 of members (RCC 39).
8. **Paying founders is itself an inurement problem.** RMO 38-2019 IV.A.1 lists "compensation, salaries, or honorarium to its trustees or organizers" as inurement. The AOI must state that trustees receive no compensation (VI.A.2.iii). Donee donor's-tax exemption requires "trustees who receive no compensation" (NIRC 101(A)(2)). Founders who are both trustees and paid researchers need a lawyer-approved structure.
9. **The "public interest" condition on scientific research** (RMO 38-2019 II.E.2.c; RR 13-98 1(g)(iii): results "available to the public on a non-discriminatory basis") conflicts with exclusive licensing of research outputs to a founder-owned company.
10. **A hybrid "stock non-profit" does not qualify for 30(E).** It is legally recognised (SEC-OGC 25-13), but Sec. 30(E) requires a non-stock corporation (St. Luke's). Its stockholders keep an interest in assets on dissolution (25-13). It is not a route to both tax exemption and founder equity.

## Open questions for counsel

1. Does the BIR accept that dividends from a Philippine for-profit subsidiary to a Sec. 30(E) non-stock parent are exempt under NIRC 27(D)(3)? Is there a BIR ruling? How are foreign-sourced dividends from a non-PH subsidiary treated?
2. How does the RMO 38-2019 operational test ("substantial part ... activities conducted for profit" means failure) fit with St. Luke's ("does not lose its tax exemption"; only the for-profit income is taxed)? What share of revenue from licensing or services is safe?
3. Can founders who are trustees (and "organizers") be paid as employees or researchers without triggering RMO IV.A.1, given RCC Sec. 29 allows pay in other capacities? Should founders who want salaries step off the board, and what does that do to control?
4. Does ML/AI research fit RMO 38-2019's definition of "scientific purposes"? How should the AOI purpose clause be worded, and should it name any income-generating activity (licensing, compute rental) so SEC's "incidental to purpose" test is met?
5. Under RCC 31-32, how can a three-trustee board approve any contract with a founder-controlled company? How many independent members and trustees are needed (RCC 91 requires trustees to be members), and does member ratification by the same founders count?
6. If Experimental Machines seeks PCNC accreditation, does RR 13-98 Sec. 10(d) bar **all** leases, sales and licences to a founder-controlled for-profit, even at fair value? Does it also cover licences of IP ("transferring its property by way of lease or sale")? What changed under EO 117 (2026)?

9. Could a merger of the nonprofit into a stock corporation (RCC 75-79) achieve what 22-14 forbids by amendment? The expected answer is no, but it should be confirmed.
10. If the for-profit is incorporated abroad (e.g., Delaware), which Philippine rules still apply to the nonprofit's investment, its dividends and any IP transfer, and does the Anti-Dummy Law or foreign-equity law matter for either entity?
